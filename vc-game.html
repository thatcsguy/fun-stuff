Section 00 — Terms of Participation

How to play

You're a venture capitalist. You have 1,000 credits. Spread them across the six startups below however you like.

RULE 01

Spend all 1,000

No holding back. Your allocations must total exactly one thousand credits.

RULE 02

Nothing, or 100+

Any investment is either zero or at least 100 credits. No 50-credit dabbling.

RULE 03

Round to tens

Every allocation is a multiple of 10. The sliders will keep you honest.

That's it. You can put everything in one startup or split it six ways.

Two things to know before you read the pitches

Every startup pays out differently, and every single one of them depends on what everybody else does. You're not solving a math problem — you're guessing what everyone else in the round is about to do.

Every startup pays a multiplier on what you put in, and it's the same multiplier for everyone holding it — nobody gets a better deal for going in bigger. A ×1.9 on a 300-credit position returns 570.

And nobody is hiding anything from you. Every rule below is the complete rule. Each pitch has the flavor on the left and the full ruleset on the right — if you only want to play, read the right-hand panels.

One extra way to score — before you read the pitches

The Diversification Grant

+300

Invest at least 100 credits in four or more startups and you get a flat bonus of 300 credits.

Not a multiplier — a flat 300 added to your final fund. No catch. Several of these startups pay most of their investors nothing. Consider this hazard pay.

Worth knowing now, because it shapes everything below: four positions of 250 each clears it, and so does 100 / 100 / 100 / 700. The tracker in the bar at the top of your screen counts your qualifying positions as you go.

The Helion Standard

“Backed by the full faith and credit of everyone else who showed up.”

The Helion is a currency. The Helion Terminal is the only object we manufacture: a brushed slab for your desk that displays your balance and nothing else. We have been asked to add a graph. We have declined.

We are aware of the lineage. There have been, by the Exchange's own count, some eleven thousand prior attempts at a currency of this kind, and eleven thousand occasions on which a founder stood roughly where we are standing and explained that this one was different. We have read their filings. They were not serious people. This is the one that becomes real money, and we would ask you to note that the argument is stronger now than it has ever been, which is precisely what makes it credible.

The more of this round's money that flows into the Helion, the more every holder earns. You want a crowd here.

Two failures concern us. Held by nearly everyone, the Helion is merely money, and money is not supposed to appreciate; our economists call that ceiling “winning.” Held by almost no one, it is a keepsake. Keepsakes are worth something. Not this much.

The rules
  • Your payout depends on Helion's share of all the money in the round — every credit everybody invests, everywhere.
  • The more Helion attracts, the higher the multiplier. You want a crowd.

The Prior

“The forecast is correct. It is correct for a limited number of people.”

Once a cycle we publish one forecast. It arrives as a single sheet, sealed, and in the lower corner it is numbered — three of three, or nine of nine. The forecast is the smaller half of what you are buying. The number is the rest of it.

We are a research house, and our research is sound, and it degrades the instant it is acted upon. A forecast read by three desks describes the coming quarter. The same forecast read by fifteen describes fifteen desks reacting to a forecast, which is a different event, and one we did not predict, and one that would not have occurred had we kept our findings to ourselves.

Our accuracy falls as our readership rises. You want to be one of the only people holding this.

We would prefer not to sell it at all. We are aware of how that reads in a prospectus. There is a ceiling on what you may commit, because a large enough position stops being a subscription and becomes a movement in the market we are describing, and we will not be made to forecast ourselves.

The rules
  • Your payout depends on how many players invest — not how much money. A reader is a reader.
  • Measured as a share of everyone who submitted. The fewer investors, the higher the multiplier.
  • Maximum stake: 250 credits.

Familiar Faces

“Why train a new employee when you can simply grow another one?”

Meet your new hire. You've met them before — that's rather the point! We grow people to order: for firms that would rather not run onboarding, for performers with more bookings than hours, and for parents who have simply run out of evenings.

Everything we do is entirely legal, in the delightful sense that nobody has got round to writing the law yet. That holds right up until we become the best-known name in the round. Come first in funding and you get hearings, protest lines outside the growth facility, and a televised afternoon with the Galactic Ethics Council, who will want to hear from the clones directly. Nothing gets banned — the orders simply stop. We would much rather be the second most interesting thing here.

And the ones already grown? Still under contract. Somebody keeps paying them, and that somebody is us.

Surface Tension

“Step four is when we announce it. Step five is when we build it.”

Watch this. That's a hull. That's fourteen thousand tonnes of nothing we never have to launch again. What we make is a bubble — a real one, an iridescent skin around a breathable volume, drifting between planets for a fraction of the price. They are beautiful. Several of them work.

The valuation doesn't climb on vehicles. It climbs on the roadmap, and the roadmap is the best part: test flight, route license, passenger service, the magazine cover, the ribbon-cutting in orbit. Every one of those is an announcement, and every announcement is a step up.

You name your exit rung in advance — reach it and you're paid at that valuation.

Here's the thing nobody says from a stage: funding is what kills us. Money buys announcements faster than engineering can catch up, and a skin only holds so many promises. More credits, earlier pop. Yours included.

The rules
  • You pick a rung, 1 to 6, when you invest. Each rung has its own multiplier.
  • Surface Tension's share of all the money in the round decides which rung it pops at.
  • Rung strictly below the pop rung → you got out in time, paid at your rung's multiplier.
  • Rung equal to or above the pop rung → you were still inside, ×0.5. The rung that pops is the rung you don't survive.
  • Fewer than 4 investors → it never climbs, everyone refunded at ×1.0. 4 investors is enough.

Windfall Securities

“Rates are published. We would like that noted.”

Windfall Securities is a game studio. It is registered as a securities firm because that is the paperwork the Founder understood, and counsel has stopped raising it. The product is STARLIGHT SEMINARY: Endless Encore, a live-service title in pre-launch, with a rarity table, a seasonal calendar, and eleven planned years of content.

Counsel is obliged to disclose the following and has been unable to establish that it is immaterial. The Founder maintains an account, in continuous good standing for eleven years, in HEARTBLOOM ACADEMY: Eternal Encore, a title in operation for four centuries and presently running its Anniversary event. He has described his own product as “that, but the part where you stop is removed.”

Every credit is another entry. Nobody's odds are good.

This round is itself structured as a draw. Five Investors will be paid and the rest receive nothing. The Founder selected this arrangement over a conventional allocation on the grounds that a conventional allocation is not exciting, and has asked whether the results can be revealed one at a time.

The rules
  • This one is a lottery, not a multiplier. Five winners take everything; everyone else gets nothing back.
  • Every credit you invest is one entry — more credits, better odds, same prize.
  • Every credit invested goes into the pot, and Windfall tops the pot up by 15%.
  • Five Investors are drawn, one at a time. Each draw is weighted by credits invested.
  • One person can't win twice — a winner is removed before the next draw.
  • Everyone else gets nothing. That's ×0.
  • Fewer than 5 investors → the draw is cancelled, everyone refunded at ×1.0. Exactly 5 investors → all five are drawn; it's just a redistribution.
  • Maximum stake: 150 credits.

The Pact

“Self-reported figures. Reviewed quarterly, by us.”

Minutes of the Pact. Attendance: six partners, six systems. Verification of any partner's figures requires a round trip of eleven to nineteen months, against a reporting period of three. A confirmed number is therefore always a number about a quarter that has already closed. Audit function: none. There was one. It rated its own performance excellent and was dissolved, and the finding could not be checked in time to matter.

Noted for the record: honest figures keep the total defensible, and a defensible total pays everyone. Inflated figures make one partner outstanding — a stronger quarter than their peers, drawn from the same total. This works beautifully so long as most partners decline to do it.

You declare REPORT or INFLATE when you invest, and nobody sees it until the end.

Should the total become indefensible, an investor will ask where it came from. The partners required to explain their methodology will be, specifically, the ones whose figures were most impressive. This item was raised and not discussed further.

The rules
  • Declare REPORT or INFLATE when you invest. Nobody sees any declaration until the end.
  • Only the number of people on each side matters — one person, one vote, no matter how much they invested.
  • At least half the Pact's investors chose REPORT → the numbers hold.
  • Otherwise — more than half chose INFLATE → the review happens.
  • Counted among Pact investors only, not everyone in the round.
  • Fewer than 4 investors → never forms, everyone refunded at ×1.0. 4 investors is enough.

Section 07 — The Tiebreaker

The Cold Read

Free, and worth no credits at all.

Highest final fund total wins. To break ties, everyone answers one more question:

What percentage of all the money in the round will the least-funded startup end up with?

You don't have to say which startup it'll be. Just the number. Somebody finishes last. With six startups and 1,000 credits each, an even split would be about 16.7% apiece — so the question is really how badly the room's least favorite gets left out. It could be a respectable 12%. It could be nothing at all, if every single person skips it.

Closest guess wins the tie. Over or under doesn't matter, only distance.
